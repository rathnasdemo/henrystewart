*Adapted from the video [Why was France so useless in World War Two?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hr_vSJVuZtg)*

I think we can all agree that World War II was not the French military's finest hour. France's collapse in the face of Hitler's war machine has made it the butt of cheap jokes ever since: "Bonjour, you cheese-eating surrender monkeys." Only six weeks passed between the first German tanks crossing the border and France signing away its freedom and dignity.

The fall of France was as astonishing then as it is now. The whole world expected France to put up much more of a fight, the Germans included. It was believed to have one of the strongest militaries in the world, and in World War I it had held out against Germany for four years. In World War II it collapsed in weeks. "Surely I must be dreaming" was how one French pilot summed it up in his memoir. One American diplomat said that never before had one event so stunned the entire world.

It would be easy to assume the Nazis won through overwhelming military superiority: more men, or better equipment. That is not the case. The French collapse was much more complicated, and much more interesting, than that.

## Six weeks in 1940

*[Watch from 1:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hr_vSJVuZtg&t=69s)*

By the late 1930s war was very clearly on the horizon. Hitler had established a totalitarian state in Germany, but that wasn't enough for him. His book *Mein Kampf* laid bare his hatred of the Jews and his belief that the German race should rule the world. Every nation in Europe was rearming as fast as it could.

War broke out on 1 September 1939, when Hitler invaded Poland and Britain and France declared war on Germany. France launched a minor offensive into Germany but withdrew after only a few days, and for months the war went quiet. Very little fighting happened in what came to be known as the Phoney War, while everyone built up their militaries for the inevitable resumption.

The plan of Britain and France was to blockade Germany with the Royal Navy and, at the same time, prepare to defend France from invasion. That invasion finally came in May 1940, a full eight months after war had been declared. Britain and France assumed it would go through Belgium, the route taken so many times throughout history, and rushed their best forces there to meet the German advance.

They were mistaken. Relatively few German forces went into Belgium. The main attack came further south, through the Ardennes forest. The French had assumed that moving huge numbers of men and vehicles through the forest would be impossible, and that the Germans would be vulnerable crossing the River Meuse. They bet the Germans wouldn't attack there, and they bet wrong.

In only three days the Germans made it through the Ardennes and across the Meuse. From there they raced towards the Channel as fast as they could, almost recklessly ignoring the threat of counterattack, and reached it seven days after crossing the river, only ten days into the invasion. For the Allies it was a monumental disaster. A huge portion of their forces was surrounded and cut off from the rest of France. Their only hope was to cut through the German corridor and break out, and they failed. The German stranglehold on the Allied pocket tightened until the Allies were forced to withdraw in the famous Dunkirk evacuation.

With that, the French will to fight collapsed. Within days they were talking about an armistice, and on 22 June the deal was done. Nazi Germany occupied the north of France, while the south became a vassal state, Vichy France. Hitler had achieved in six weeks what Germany had failed to achieve in four years of fighting in World War I.

## Was Germany simply stronger?

*[Watch from 4:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hr_vSJVuZtg&t=270s)*

If France fell in only six weeks, it would be reasonable to assume it was down to overwhelming German superiority: more men, more and better tanks and planes. I want to persuade you that that really isn't the case, and that the two militaries were fairly comparable.

**Infantry.** The Germans had 3 million men available for the invasion. Roughly half had had only a few weeks of training, and half were over 40. Only 10% were motorised; the other 90% got around on foot. For those 3 million men there were only 120,000 vehicles, and logistics were mostly horse-drawn. France had 2.2 million men with 300,000 vehicles for transport, more than twice as many as the Germans. France also had allies: Britain had 200,000 troops in France, all of them motorised, and Belgium and the Netherlands contributed perhaps up to a million men between them.

**Tanks.** Here too the French could absolutely match the Germans. France had about 3,000 modern tanks, slightly more than Germany's 2,500 or so. Quality is hard to compare, with so many factors to weigh (size, speed, armour, firepower, manoeuvrability), but most historians agree the French had the best all-round tank, the SOMUA S35. It was fast, well protected, and had greater firepower than its German equivalent, the Panzer III. The French also had the best heavy tank, the B1, with more firepower than the German heavy tank, the Panzer IV, and armour twice as thick. The B1 did have weaknesses: it was half the speed of the Panzer IV, and it consumed so much fuel that it could only operate for around four hours.

**Artillery.** If France had a slight advantage in tanks, it had a significant one in artillery: about 11,000 guns to Germany's 8,000, with a greater proportion of powerful large-calibre guns. Its weaknesses were anti-tank weapons, with only 270 of its best anti-tank gun and many units relying on an outdated model that was ineffective against modern tanks, and anti-aircraft guns, with only about 4,000 to Germany's 9,000. Some units even had to use light machine guns against aircraft.

**Air power.** This was where France had a significant disadvantage. Throughout the 1930s French aircraft production disappointed. France tried to build multi-purpose aircraft that could act as bombers, fighters and reconnaissance planes when what it really needed were aircraft specialised for one job, and it struggled to raise production. In 1936 the German air force overtook the French in both quality and quantity. The French knew it: France's air chief of staff warned the government that in a war the French air force would be destroyed in a few days.

In March 1938 the government finally prioritised the air force, and 42% of military spending went on building planes and training pilots. It did begin to work: between November 1938 and September 1939, production went from 41 planes a month to 300. Unfortunately, it was all a bit too late. When the invasion came, France still didn't have enough planes, and many it did have were outdated. France had designed a good fighter, the Dewoitine D.520, as good as the best German fighters, but production only began in December 1939, so just 80 were available. However you count the numbers, Germany had almost three times as many planes, and the roughly 400 contributed by Britain came nowhere near closing the gap.

Overall, then, I think the Germans did have an advantage in military strength, but it wasn't overwhelming, and it certainly can't explain why France fell in six weeks.

What people said beforehand backs that up. "The French army is stronger than ever before in its history," declared one French general. Other nations agreed. The military editor of the *New York Times* wrote: "The French army has long been called by experts the best in the world and its finest divisions are undoubtedly unmatched." Even the Germans believed France had a first-rate military. One early German plan forecast that it would cost half a million German soldiers just to push the Allies back to the Somme. When Hitler demanded a fast, decisive attack like the one dealt to Poland, he was told that wouldn't be possible against a military as powerful as France's.

So if it wasn't overwhelming strength, what was it?

## The Ardennes: the attack France never planned for

*[Watch from 10:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hr_vSJVuZtg&t=618s)*

The importance of the German offensive through the Ardennes really can't be overstated. The Germans got from their own territory all the way to the English Channel in just ten days. I find that absolutely insane. I don't think the war had to be lost from that point, and I'll come back to that, but it was an enormous setback. So how on earth did they do it?

To say the French weren't prepared for an attack through the Ardennes is an enormous understatement. They did all their military planning with absolutely zero attention paid to the idea that the Germans would attack there. In fairness, they had reasons. The region was hilly and forested with a poor road network, so moving large numbers of men and vehicles through it would be incredibly difficult, and once through, an army would still have to cross the Meuse while vulnerable. Ever since the Romans, armies moving between France and Germany had gone through Belgium, whose plains were the easiest to traverse and offered the shortest route into France.

In March 1934 one French general declared the area impenetrable: "Any enemy that attacks there will be pincered as he leaves the forest. It's not a vulnerable sector." In the words of another, the Ardennes had never favoured large operations.

So France planned to rush its best forces into Belgium as soon as the fighting started. Having fought World War I largely on French soil, it was eager to keep as much of the fighting in Belgium as possible. Expecting the invasion there, it left the Ardennes only minimally defended, by poorly trained and poorly equipped forces. One of the generals tasked with defending the Ardennes complained, and his pleas were ignored. A government report in March, two months before the invasion, warned of "grave insufficiencies" in the French defences there. A different general responded furiously that no urgent measures were needed.

By going through the Ardennes, the Germans were breaking with history. Their plan was bold and extremely risky, and even some German commanders doubted it would work; some thought it would be a disaster. The German chief of staff wrote that it had only a 10% chance of success. The doubts were understandable. Getting a massive army across an area with such a limited road network was an extremely difficult logistical operation, and it had to be done fast, before France could react. If German vehicles sat bumper to bumper on the roads of the Ardennes for days, they would be sitting ducks for Allied bombs and artillery. How quickly could they get through? One commander thought four days, others nine. Everything hinged on that question.

## Too slow to see it, too slow to react

*[Watch from 13:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hr_vSJVuZtg&t=819s)*

So France made a terrible error in not defending the Ardennes. But surely, once the attack was under way, it quickly realised what was happening and moved to counter it?

No. The French were incredibly slow to work out what the Germans were doing. The attack began at 4:30 on 10 May, as 130,000 soldiers and 1,600 vehicles began to traverse the Ardennes forest: in the words of one German commander, "the greatest traffic jam known to that date in Europe". French air reconnaissance did notice considerable motorised and armoured forces on the move, including a significant amount of bridging equipment. Still the penny didn't drop. One French commander in the area didn't request any extra bomber support; another French general did order bombing, but was overruled. Most German units moving through the Ardennes didn't meet a single French soldier. Those French forces that did meet the advance were heavily outnumbered and quickly fell back, and despite meeting far greater numbers than expected, they still didn't realise this was the main attack.

The first German units reached the Meuse within two days, and once again the French had to guess the German plan. Where would they cross? The French commander thought it would be further south and stationed his best units there. He assumed the town of Sedan wasn't vulnerable because of natural obstacles in the river, so he stationed his weakest troops there: a division of Series B reservists. Not only were they poorly trained, the entire division had only one anti-aircraft gun. Once again the French had completely misjudged the Germans. Sedan was one of their main targets.

The attack began at 7 a.m. on 13 May, three days into the invasion. For eight hours German dive bombers pounded the French positions, and the French troops, unable to fight back, became very demoralised. Then the Germans started to cross the river. Had the French had even reasonable forces in the area, they could have inflicted significant losses and potentially pushed the Germans back. The Germans were crossing in dinghies that could barely stay afloat under the weight of their equipment; they were extremely vulnerable. The French inflicted some casualties, but not enough. The Germans crossed the river and took the French bunkers. One squad of 11 Germans took seven French bunkers. By 5:30 p.m., less than 11 hours after the assault began, the Germans were building their first bridge across the river.

Through the afternoon some French soldiers began to flee their positions, which gradually turned into full-blown panic. By the end of 13 May the Germans had crossed the river at Sedan and in two other places. Only now, three days into the invasion, did the penny begin to drop. France realised what the German plan was and how ill-prepared it was to fight it. At French headquarters there was an atmosphere of desperation. One French commander exclaimed, "There has been a collapse," and burst into tears.

## Defeatism, dithering and a fraying alliance

*[Watch from 17:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hr_vSJVuZtg&t=1023s)*

We can already see several major problems. The first was France's total inability to guess the German plans. It was wrong that the Germans would attack through Belgium, and wrong about where they would cross the river. How much of that was terrible judgement and how much bad luck is debatable, but it certainly cost France dearly. The French should have paid more attention to the possibility that the Germans would do something unexpected, and to which German strategies would leave them most vulnerable.

The reaction at headquarters shows a second problem: a defeatist mentality and low morale. The Germans crossing the river in three places was indeed a major setback, but it wasn't the end of the world. Those bridgeheads were very small and vulnerable to counterattack.

That brings us to the third problem: inflexibility. The French were completely unable to react to German movements and mount an effective counterattack. First, they dithered. The counterattack should have started immediately, on the evening of the 13th, but it didn't start until the next morning, partly because French doctrine said offensives should be carefully planned in advance. While the French dithered, the Germans were frantically getting as many men and tanks across the river as they could, and by the time the counterattack came, they had enough to fight it off. French infantry divisions had disintegrated and fled. The Allies tried bombing the Germans, but with an acute shortage of bombers they had to use outdated aircraft, achieved little and suffered heavy losses. Divisions of heavy tanks were sent into the fray and also achieved little.

With the Germans established on the French side of the river, France again showed its inability to guess their plans. It assumed they would move south to outflank the Maginot Line, and sent many of its reinforcements south to guard against that. In fact, the Germans were going to charge north-west to the Channel as fast as they could, cutting off the Allied forces in Belgium from the rest of France. It was a bold and risky plan: advancing that fast would leave their flanks incredibly vulnerable to counterattack, and with the corridor so thin, the men at the front could easily be surrounded themselves. Despite the risk, the Germans went for it. The best example is when Erwin Rommel advanced 110 kilometres in a single day, pushing far ahead of his infantry and artillery. Against a more effective enemy that should have been hugely reckless and dangerous. Instead, it demoralised the French, who were stunned at how far west the Germans had got. There was even disagreement among the German commanders about whether to continue the breakneck advance. The main commander wanted to slow down and consolidate; one of his subordinates threatened to resign if that happened, and so the advance continued.

The Allies saw the Germans' vulnerability. As Churchill put it, "the tortoise has protruded its head dangerously far from its shell." That was all very well, but if the French couldn't get their forces into position fast, it would count for nothing, and at that point it didn't look likely. The Allied forces were essentially collapsing. Those in Belgium were falling back to avoid encirclement, and the rapid German advance had disrupted Allied communications so badly that nobody truly knew what was going on. Some small counterattacks had moderate success, but they needed far more force concentrated on a specific point to be decisive. There was a plan to release a whole load of units from around the Maginot Line and attack north, but as ever there was dithering and delay.

Morale and defeatism remained a big problem. On the fifth day of the invasion, French Prime Minister Paul Reynaud told Churchill: "We have been defeated. We are beaten. We have lost the battle. The front is broken near Sedan." Another French general said to a British major: "I'm shattered and I can't do anything against these Panzers."

On 20 May, only ten days after the invasion began, the Germans reached the Channel and the Allied forces in Belgium were cut off. The only hope was to break out of the encirclement by attacking the German column from the north and the south at the same time. That meant coordinating the forces in France with those in Belgium, many of them British or Belgian, which brings us to the fourth problem: the Allies' relationships with each other. There was a good deal of mutual suspicion. The French often doubted British commitment to defending France, and suspected the Belgians of pro-German sympathies. There were communication problems, misunderstandings and disagreements, and all of it cost valuable time they absolutely couldn't afford.

The breakout plan shows this clearly. The French supreme commander, Weygand, flew to Ypres to organise the offensive, but the Allies couldn't get all their major commanders in the same place at the same time. Weygand's first meeting was with King Leopold of Belgium, who wasn't particularly enthusiastic about the plan and demanded to hear from the British commander before agreeing to anything. Weygand came away thinking the Belgians weren't going to be much use, and flew back to Paris unsure whether the plan would go ahead. An hour after he left, Gort, the British commander, arrived. Gort was told the plan, wasn't hugely optimistic about its chances, but agreed to go ahead.

French morale was still an enormous problem. The British Chief of Staff visited the French commanders, and this was his description:

> I then found Billotte and Blanchard in a state of complete desperation. No plan, no thought of a plan. Ready to be slaughtered. I lost my temper and shook Billotte by the button of his tunic. The man is completely beaten.

He did get the two French generals to agree to the plan: Allied forces would attack the town of Arras from the north and the south simultaneously. Even then they delayed. General Altmayer, who was to command the offensive, said the forces wouldn't be ready for two days. "One should see things as they are," he said. "The troops have buggered off." He then said he was ready to go and get himself killed at the head of a battalion.

Despite this patheticness from the French commanders, the attempted breakout went ahead on 21 May, the 11th day of the invasion. And, you might want to be seated for this, it was actually quite successful. The Allies took Arras. There was a glimmer of hope. The Germans were pretty rattled: Rommel lost more tanks that day than on any other. In some ways that isn't surprising, because the Germans were overextended to an enormous degree.

So what happened next was that the Allies threw everything they had into pressing their advantage as hard and fast as they could, right? No. They did what they had been doing all along: dithering, hesitating and arguing. None of the commanders wanted their own men to lead the offensive. After several days, holding Arras became untenable for the small British force there, and it was forced to withdraw. In Paris, the withdrawal was read as Britain abandoning the breakout. The French convinced themselves that the British weren't committed to defending France and were buggering off back home.

Relations between France and Britain were now really bad. Weygand thought the British government was lying to him, and told the British Chief of Staff he would like to box his ears. One can't help thinking: if only that sort of fighting spirit had been applied to the Nazi army invading his country.

The Germans were now threatening to take the entire coastline, which would leave the British and French forces unable to evacuate by sea. So the Allies abandoned the plan to attack south and retreated north. The problem of being a coalition surfaced again a few days later, when Belgium requested a ceasefire with Germany. Britain and France were furious. The French prime minister said in a radio broadcast that never in history had there been such a betrayal, which does strike me as somewhat of an exaggeration.

## Dunkirk, and the end of the will to fight

*[Watch from 25:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hr_vSJVuZtg&t=1547s)*

By now it was clear that the forces in the north would have to be evacuated from Dunkirk. Given that so much up to this point had been such a farce, you might be surprised to hear that the evacuation was carried out really quite competently. In the end about 340,000 Allied troops were evacuated, and about 35,000 were left behind and taken prisoner.

But while it was relatively successful in terms of men evacuated, it brought relations between Britain and France to a new low. The French felt the British had prioritised British soldiers for evacuation and hadn't provided as much air support as they could have. Weygand said: "Every people has their virtues and defects. The Englishman is most motivated by almost instinctive selfishness." The British in turn now had very little respect for the French. One British commander wrote: "There hardly seems to be any mistake that the French do not make. Their generals are beneath contempt." That complete breakdown made it virtually impossible for the Allies to keep fighting together.

Theoretically, the battle still wasn't lost after Dunkirk. France still had much of its army intact, and the Germans controlled only a small part of the country. In reality the French had very little will left to fight, and their forces were in disarray. They were now outnumbered, and the Germans made fast progress into the centre of France. On 10 June 1940 the government announced that it was leaving the capital, and very soon it was talking about surrender.

That was understandable, but it wasn't the only option. Surrender made sense if the war was over and the British were going to give up too, which the French assumed they would. But Britain was led by Churchill, who wasn't exactly the giving-up type. If the French had had some of that Churchillian spirit, there were options. The government could have gone abroad, either to French North Africa or to Britain, taken as many military resources as possible and continued the struggle from there. In theory it could have kept a guerrilla campaign going on the mainland, supported by Britain. It should be said that this would have led to enormous destruction, and given the brutality of the Nazis, I wouldn't put it past them to literally execute everyone in France. By agreeing to a ceasefire, some French leaders hoped to keep some control over a reasonable amount of France.

On 22 June, when the Germans had taken about two-thirds of France, the ceasefire was signed. The north came under direct Nazi occupation, while the south became a German puppet state, Vichy France. Vichy collaborated with Nazi Germany to a shameful degree, and many French people bought fully into Nazi ideology. Vichy France became the only unoccupied nation to actively round up and deport its Jews to be murdered in the Holocaust. Antisemitism had been strong in France before the war, and many people in Vichy were totally on board with the extermination of the Jews. Some brave French people kept fighting as part of the Resistance, and a government in exile was set up in London, led by Charles de Gaulle. France remained under Nazi occupation for four years, until it was liberated after D-Day.

## Why France fell

*[Watch from 28:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hr_vSJVuZtg&t=1729s)*

If I were forced to name the biggest factors in France's stunning collapse, I would say these four:

1. **The failure to defend the Ardennes.**
2. **Inflexibility:** the failure to react to the Germans going through the Ardennes, and to counterattack once they had crossed the river.
3. **Low morale and defeatism.**
4. **The inherent challenges of being part of a coalition.**

For me, the first two are the biggest. France really wouldn't have needed to do much to make the route through the Ardennes and the crossing of the Meuse incredibly difficult and costly for the Germans. Simply destroying the road network would have cost them days. France could have laid mines along the route. With even moderate amounts of artillery or bombers, it could have inflicted huge losses.

Even without any of that, if France had reacted fast, it could have devastated the Germans. Had the French been able to counterattack once the Germans crossed the Meuse and drive them back to the other side of the river, the Germans would have been absolutely helpless. A huge amount of the German military would have been trapped in the Ardennes forest, barely able to fire back and unable to evacuate quickly. Don't forget that a German commander described it as the biggest traffic jam in history. That enormous mass of men and vehicles would have been the easiest target in the world for artillery, and remember, the French had more artillery than the Germans. It would have been an absolute killing field.

It was precisely because of that possibility that the Germans were so nervous about their plan. Unfortunately for France, and for the world, that's not what happened.
