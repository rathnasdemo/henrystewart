*Adapted from the video [Who was truly to blame for World War One? (Not the Germans!)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=37QFb-LRd9Q)*

If you held a competition to rank the stupidest things humanity has ever done, World War I would be a strong contender for the winner. For me, few events in history are as depressing as its outbreak, not only for the war's insane body count but for the sheer pointlessness of it. Sometimes you just have to wonder what on earth they were all fighting for.

In 1914 Europe had been relatively peaceful for a century. It was the absolute centre of the world: the richest and most prosperous place on Earth, driving innovation and inventing astonishing new technologies. Then, for no reason at all, Europe decided to tear itself apart and destroy what it had spent so long creating.

The outbreak of the war was tragic, but it was also complicated, unlikely and frankly weird. How does an act of terrorism by a Serbian nationalist in Bosnia end with Germany invading poor old Belgium? Many of Europe's leaders were desperate to prevent war and devastated when they failed. When the German ambassador handed Germany's declaration of war to Russia, he burst into tears. The British prime minister also cried on the eve of war.

The strange sequence of events that led to World War I has occupied historians since before the war was even over, and so has the fierce debate over who exactly was to blame. That is the question I want to address here. It goes without saying that nothing definitive can be said; historians who have spent years studying it disagree. What we can do is recount the main actions of each nation before the war and try to judge whether, on balance, they escalated or de-escalated the crisis.

## The July Crisis in brief

*[Watch from 1:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=37QFb-LRd9Q&t=114s)*

In June 1914 Europe was at peace, and war didn't seem to be on the horizon. There were tensions, of course, and particularly strong ones between Austria and Serbia, which had conflicting claims over land in the Balkans. Serbia had a strong nationalist movement that wanted a bigger, grander Serbia including all the ethnic Serbs of the region. The Austro-Hungarian Empire was very weak and frankly barely holding itself together, and many people thought it might break apart at any minute. Should that happen, Serbia wanted to be in a position to take as much territory as possible.

Serbia was particularly hacked off about something Austria had done six years earlier. Austria had been administering Bosnia, which on paper was part of the incredibly weak Ottoman Empire. In 1908 the world was stunned when Austria-Hungary annexed Bosnia outright. Serbia was incensed, and some Serbian nationalists even called for war. Tensions persisted, but Europe just about held itself together.

Then came the assassination. On 28 June 1914, in Bosnia, a Serbian nationalist assassinated the heir to the Austro-Hungarian Empire. That was absolutely huge; perhaps a modern equivalent would be 9/11. Austria was outraged, but unsure how to respond. Should it go to war with Serbia? If not, what should it do? Germany was allied with Austria and promised to back it whatever it chose. Russia was allied with Serbia and promised to defend it if Austria went to war.

Eventually Austria decided to send Serbia an ultimatum: accept a series of humiliating demands or be invaded. It was sent on 23 July, almost four weeks after the assassination. The Serbians agreed to some, but not all, of the terms, and five days later Austria declared war. The next day it began shelling the Serbian capital.

Meanwhile Russia, fearing attack from both Austria and Germany, had been readying its military. That process, known as mobilisation, was hugely provocative to Germany, which issued its own ultimatum demanding that Russia stop. Russia refused, and Germany declared war. That brought in another great power, France, which was allied with Russia and so also had to declare war on Germany. Threatened on both sides, Germany decided to attack France first, hoping for a quick victory that would let it turn its military towards Russia. Attacking France meant going through neutral Belgium, and that provoked Britain. After German troops advanced into Belgium, Britain declared war.

That's the whistle-stop tour. Now let's look at what each country contributed to that astonishing escalation, from a local, contained conflict to the most horrific war the world had ever seen.

## Serbia

*[Watch from 4:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=37QFb-LRd9Q&t=299s)*

Serbia's main culpability is, of course, its role in the assassination of Franz Ferdinand. Working out exactly how involved the Serbian state was is extremely difficult. It's almost certainly not the case that the Serbian prime minister ordered the assassination, but some members of the Serbian state, particularly the military, were involved. Since everything was done in secret, determining exactly who knew what is impossible.

The assassination was carried out with the help of a movement called the Black Hand, also known as Unification or Death, and the Serbian state had ties to it. The Black Hand was formed to unite all the territories with ethnic Slav populations into one nation. Much as Germany and Italy had recently been unified into single states, it hoped to do the same for the southern Slavs, creating something like Yugoslavia. One of the biggest barriers to that project was the Austrian Empire, which is why tensions were so high.

The relationship between the Black Hand and the Serbian state was complex and opaque. High-ranking Serbian military officials were heavily involved in the Black Hand and likely involved in the assassination. The Black Hand had helped bring the king to power 11 years earlier and still had significant influence over the royal family; one member was the Serbian prince's tutor. Given those military ties, the weapons used in the assassination likely came from the Serbian military. Because of all that, it was, and still is, hard to judge whether the assassination should be considered an act of state aggression.

Needless to say, it was extremely inflammatory to Austria, which knew Serbia's hands weren't entirely clean. The Serbian government offered the usual formal sympathy, but the Serbian public was elated, even euphoric, and celebrated openly. The Serbian press fanned the flames, praising the assassins and accusing the Austrians of lying, and the most inflammatory headlines were reported in the Austrian press, causing huge anger. The Serbian prime minister said inflammatory things too: the day after the assassination, he said that if Austria exploited it, Serbia wouldn't hesitate to defend itself.

Perhaps even more inflammatory was Serbia's failure to investigate the assassination properly. People from many nations, from *The Times* of London to King Carol of Romania, agreed that a thorough investigation should happen. It didn't. The Serbian authorities never carried out an investigation appropriate to the importance of the assassination. One was started, and a week later it was closed, with the finding that the assassins had nothing to do with Serbia. For Austria, that was a slap in the face. Perhaps the Serbians were so reluctant precisely because a real investigation would have shown links to the Serbian elite.

Eventually, of course, Austria sent its ultimatum, which raises the question of how to judge Serbia's response. Historians debate how conciliatory it was. Some see it as giving Austria almost everything it wanted; others see it as largely a rejection. In the words of the historian Christopher Clark, it was "a highly perfumed rejection on most points". Some at the time did see it as conciliatory. The British foreign secretary said Serbia had given satisfaction to a degree he never would have believed possible. Kaiser Wilhelm famously said: "Any reason for war has now been eliminated."

Overall, though, the Serbs were clearly open to war, at least with Austria. At the same time as they delivered their response to the ultimatum, they ordered a general mobilisation. Their openness is clear from what was being said at the end of July. The Serbian ambassador in Russia was excited to report that Russia was mobilising 1.7 million men:

> In my opinion, this presents to us a splendid opportunity to use this event wisely and achieve the full unification of the Serbs. It is desirable, therefore, that Austria-Hungary should attack us.

Serbia's actions were extremely inflammatory, and made it very difficult for Austria not to respond militarily.

## Austria-Hungary

*[Watch from 9:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=37QFb-LRd9Q&t=576s)*

Assessing Austria's culpability hinges on one big question: what would have been a reasonable response to the assassination of Franz Ferdinand?

Remember how big a deal it was. Franz Ferdinand would very soon have become emperor. At the time, royalty was seen as more than people. They were the living embodiment of the state, the flag in flesh and blood. For people in Austria, the attack on their royalty was an attack on them personally. Combine that with how provocatively Serbia behaved afterwards, and you can see why the Austrians were indignant with rage. Personally, I find it very difficult to decide what a reasonable response would have been. To expect Austria to do nothing doesn't seem reasonable, but neither does a full-on invasion and occupation.

So what were members of the Austrian government saying? They were almost entirely in favour of war with Serbia. The chief of staff of the military famously said: "If you have a poison adder at your heel, you stamp on its head. You don't wait for it to bite." The minister of war declared: "War is the only way out of the current predicament." The German ambassador in Vienna reported that almost everyone he spoke to wanted, in his words, "a thorough settling of accounts with Serbia".

The Austrian foreign minister, Leopold Berchtold, had been relatively anti-war with Serbia in previous crises. After the assassination that changed, and he saw war as the only option. "Austria's policy of patience has exhausted its plausibility," he said. Partly, the Austrians were worried about looking weak:

> If Austria shows weakness, our neighbours to the south and east will become even more confident of our impotence and pursue their work of destruction with ever more determination.

All that said, Austria did offer Serbia the ultimatum, which theoretically offered a route to peace. But remember that it was designed to be rejected. To a large degree it was an attempt by Austria not to look like the aggressor. How reasonable its terms were is debatable. The most contentious part was clause five, which demanded that Austrian police be allowed onto Serbian soil to investigate the assassination. That would have been difficult for any nation to accept. On the other hand, given that Serbia's own investigation had been such a sham, it's understandable that Austria demanded it.

Overall, then, the Austrians were very up for war with Serbia. But the disaster of World War I is that it went so far beyond a war between Austria and Serbia. How much blame does Austria bear for escalating it to the other powers, particularly Russia?

What's interesting is how little the Austrians considered the prospect of a wider European war. It was discussed occasionally, but it wasn't a big part of their decision-making. That might be because they had Germany's backing and were very confident in the German military: for Austria, Germany could either act as a deterrent or win the war for them. Or perhaps they were simply so convinced they were morally in the right that they assumed other countries would side with them.

So can Austria be blamed for the escalation? The fact that it wasn't really considering a big European war at least implies it wasn't looking for one. On the other hand, it can be criticised for not foreseeing what would happen. If it had spent more time considering how things might escalate, it might have acted differently, and war might have been prevented.

Also worth mentioning is what Austria did six years earlier: the annexation of Bosnia. It caused an outcry from all the major European nations and the Balkan states, and it was one of the biggest reasons relations between Austria and Serbia were so bad. It also dramatically worsened relations between Austria and Russia. The annexation was such a big deal that it almost caused a major war: Serbia even mobilised its military. In the end Russia and Serbia were forced to back down, and Austria appeared to have got everything it wanted. But it had dramatically ramped up tensions.

## Franz, darling, what were you doing?

*[Watch from 13:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=37QFb-LRd9Q&t=825s)*

While we're talking about Austria, it's worth mentioning the culpability of the man himself. It can be summed up with a simple question. Franz, darling: on 28 June 1914, what on earth were you doing?

It's impossible to overstate how irresponsible Franz Ferdinand's actions were that day. First, he was in Bosnia, which had been very controversially annexed only six years earlier. That was obviously a slap in the face for the Serbs. Then there was the date. The parade took place on 28 June, which was, and still is, the anniversary of the Battle of Kosovo, one of the most important dates in the Serbian calendar. That was a second slap in the face for Serbia, at a time when relations were extremely tense. Then he went out in an open-top car, along a route published in advance. It's as if they were trying to make an assassination as easy as possible.

Finally, if all that wasn't enough, he didn't leave after the first assassination attempt. That's right: the shooting of Franz Ferdinand was actually the second attempt on his life that day. The first, with a bomb, failed to kill him. If you're a royal visiting a controversial city and someone tries to assassinate you, you might think the sensible thing to do would be to leave as fast as possible. That's not what Franz did. He got back in the same open-top car and went to visit the people wounded by the bomb. The second, successful attempt happened on his way to the hospital.

The sheer insanity of some of those decisions is breathtaking. When you're that important to a country, you have an obligation not to act recklessly and put your life in danger. Your life isn't just your own; you have a responsibility to your nation and its people. Franz Ferdinand did, of course, pay a heavy price for his mistake. Europe then paid for it too, to the tune of tens of millions of lives.

## Russia

*[Watch from 15:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=37QFb-LRd9Q&t=959s)*

In the immediate aftermath of the assassination, Russia did various things to inflame the situation. First, it gave Austria almost no sympathy and denied that Serbia had anything to do with it. The Russian Foreign Office claimed there was no Serbian involvement at a time when Austria had already produced evidence to the contrary.

The Russians also lied about what was happening in Serbia. The Russian ambassador in Serbia claimed Austria was lying about the Serbian celebrations: according to him, "the entire Serbian nation was moved to sympathy by the appalling murders at Sarajevo". That was simply untrue. He also claimed that underground networks like the Black Hand didn't exist, a distasteful irony given that members of the Russian state itself were involved with it.

Essentially, the Russians were accusing the Austrians of fabricating evidence, and implying that Austria was using the assassination as a pretext to attack Serbia because it wanted to annex it. According to Russia, Austria didn't have the right to do anything in response. The Russians wouldn't even accept Austrian demands for an investigation in Serbia. When the Austrian ambassador merely mentioned that Austria might ask Serbia to help with one, the Russian foreign minister warned him that it would make a very bad impression in Russia, and that Austria should drop the idea "lest they set foot upon a dangerous path".

Something else that massively fanned the flames was a newspaper article published on 13 June, headlined "We are ready. France must be ready too". It was widely reprinted in the French and German press, and, most egregiously, the Russian war minister had been involved in publishing it. It painted a picture of a mighty Russian war machine ready to fall upon Germany. The Russian army would soon have 2.3 million men, more than Germany and Austria combined, and thanks to the rail network, mobilisation times were plummeting. Naturally, the Germans were extremely alarmed. The Kaiser wrote:

> Ha, at last the Russians have placed their cards on the table. Anyone in Germany who doesn't believe that Russia and France are working towards an imminent war with us belongs in the Dalldorf asylum.

The German chancellor wrote that previously only extremists had believed Russia wanted war; now even calm politicians were starting to believe it.

When Austria sent its ultimatum to Serbia, the Russian foreign minister accused Austria of wanting war and said it was setting Europe on fire. Russia's response was a mixture of escalation and de-escalation. On the one hand, it asked Austria to extend the time limit of the ultimatum, and told Serbia to withdraw its armed forces from the border and avoid any unnecessary provocation. On the other, it began stockpiling weapons and preparing to mobilise.

The Russians were determined to take a "firm approach". The foreign minister stated that Russia would not remain inactive if the dignity and integrity of the Serb people, "brothers in blood", were under threat. In the words of the agriculture minister, Russia must take "a firmer and more energetic attitude towards the unreasonable claims of the Central Powers". That attitude came partly from a belief among Russia's leaders that they had been too conciliatory in previous crises. They saw Russia's conduct during the Balkan Wars and the Bosnian crisis as passive, and felt it had got them nowhere. Now Russia had to make a stand; if it didn't, it would lose all credibility with its allies.

Other Russian actions made it less likely that Serbia would accept Austria's demands. The Tsar urged the Serbs to "fight like lions". The foreign minister told Serbia not to accept a British offer of mediation. And something that dramatically affected Serbia's response was Russia's decision to mobilise. Serbia had been considering accepting the ultimatum, but once it knew of Russia's support, it wasn't going to.

### Mobilisation: the decisive step

*[Watch from 20:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=37QFb-LRd9Q&t=1207s)*

Russia's decision to mobilise was by far its biggest contribution to the escalation, and its significance can't be overstated. Today it's easy to think that preparing your military is reasonable, and that having an army ready doesn't necessarily mean a country is going to attack. But given the military thinking of the time, mobilisation was extremely provocative.

It was generally believed that whichever nation got its army to the battlefield first would win. In the Franco-Prussian War, for example, Prussia defeated France in large part by getting its army into the field first. So when Russia mobilised, it was very difficult for Germany not to, for fear that Russia would strike first and win a decisive victory. The game theory created incentives that made war harder to avoid.

Russia was the first nation to order a general mobilisation. Even Austria-Hungary was only partially mobilised against Serbia at that point, and the German government hadn't yet declared a "state of imminent danger of war", the level below mobilisation.

We can see how provocative a step it was from what the Russians did after war broke out. In the records the Russian government produced, they edited the date of Austria's general mobilisation, moving it three days earlier so that Russia's mobilisation would look like a reaction. A telegram from the Russian ambassador in Vienna was edited too. Originally it said the order for general mobilisation was expected the following day, 30 July; it was changed to say the order had already been signed. In truth, Austria went into general mobilisation on 1 August, a full two days after Russia. The Russians knew that their decision to mobilise was a big step on the road to war and increased their culpability. Once it was done, the chances of avoiding war were minimal.

Britain's response shows the same thing. On the night of 31 July, the German embassy informed the British that, in response to the Russian mobilisation, Germany had declared the state of imminent danger of war. If Russia didn't immediately reverse its mobilisation, Germany would mobilise its own forces, which would mean war. The British prime minister rushed to Buckingham Palace to wake the King, who was to send a telegram to the Tsar urging him to stop. Just think about what that means. The Germans, who would end up as Britain's enemy, told the British that Russia was mobilising and that it was a threat, and the British responded by urging Russia to stop. That was essentially an admission that the Germans were right, and that the mobilisation was indeed a huge escalation.

The Russians didn't stop, even when the Germans asked them to. Germany said that if Russia continued to mobilise, it would be forced to do the same. Given that Germany wasn't mobilising and was asking Russia not to, that seems like a reasonable request to me. It was Russia's determination to mobilise that eventually caused war between Germany and Russia. Germany sent Russia an ultimatum demanding that it stop mobilising, and when Russia refused, Germany declared war.

## Germany

*[Watch from 23:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=37QFb-LRd9Q&t=1411s)*

That brings us to the Germans. From the moment of Franz Ferdinand's assassination, the German leadership was almost entirely in favour of war between Austria and Serbia. It's easy to read that as escalation driven by a desire for war. But it can also be seen as an attempt to localise and contain the conflict.

If Austria had attacked Serbia in the immediate aftermath of the assassination, it would have been clear that it was responding directly and had no ulterior motives. Nobody would have had time to fret and grow more and more anxious about what everyone else was up to. And sympathy for Austria was at its height immediately after the assassination, so it would have been harder for any other nation to come to Serbia's aid. There may well have been some merit to that. Instead, Austria sent its ultimatum four weeks after the assassination, which gave all the nations of Europe time to wind themselves up with anxiety. Most importantly, it gave Russia time to work itself up into believing that Germany was driving everything because it wanted war. The Germans were confident that if Austria acted fast, the whole episode could be kept between Austria and Serbia, and they may well have been right.

Many German quotes show that they wanted the conflict contained and assumed it would be, while acknowledging the potential for a wider war. On 12 July the German foreign secretary wrote:

> We need to see to localizing the conflict between Austria and Serbia. Whether this is possible will depend on Russia. I have no wish for a preventative war, but if the fight offers itself, we dare not flinch.

On 21 July the chancellor wrote:

> We urgently desire a localization of the conflict. An intervention by any other power will, in view of the divergent alliance commitments, lead to incalculable consequences.

Kaiser Wilhelm II was in favour of immediate Austrian action against Serbia. When he read that his ambassador in Austria had been urging calm, he wrote:

> Who authorized him to do so? This is utterly stupid. It's none of his business, since it is entirely Austria's affair to decide what she intends to do. It's high time a clean sweep was made of the Serbs.

On another occasion Wilhelm remarked that he expected serious action by Austria against Serbia, but that it might well bring about a serious European complication. On the whole, though, he assumed Russia wouldn't intervene, for two main reasons. First, he believed Tsar Nicholas would be so shocked by the assassination of a royal that he wouldn't support Serbia. As he told a member of the navy, he did not believe there would be any further military complications, since the Tsar "would not place himself on the side of regicides". Second, he thought Russia wasn't ready for war and so would avoid it. "For the moment," he told one ambassador, "Russia gives no cause for anxiety. For the next six years, one need fear nothing from that quarter."

At times Wilhelm was less bellicose. When he saw Serbia's response to the ultimatum, he famously said that Serbia had tendered "a capitulation of the most humiliating kind" and that "any reason for war has now been eliminated". He even wanted the Austrians to be told that the case for war had vanished and that he himself was willing to mediate a peace with Serbia. Unfortunately, the message was never delivered: other members of the government didn't pass it on. Only once the Russians had mobilised did the German chancellor panic and send the Austrians a message trying to restrain them. By then, of course, it was too late.

Overall, Germany's message to Austria was that it would back it whatever it chose to do, which has come to be known as the blank cheque. Given that both were set on a military response to Serbia, you do have to wonder what exactly they hoped to achieve. War with Serbia was not going to bring the Austrian heir back from the dead, and trying to occupy Serbia in the long term would almost certainly have been a disaster. I think they probably hoped to extract some humiliating concessions and re-establish their prestige; if they looked strong, it would deter future attacks. The Austrians even acknowledged among themselves that they had no clear objective. At one point an Austrian ambassador in Germany was asked what Austria planned to do after defeating Serbia. Not wanting to give away that Austria had no plan, he simply made something up: Serbia would be partitioned. That wasn't Austria's official plan, and the ambassador had no authority to say it.

As with Austria, there's the question of how much Germany was to blame for bringing Russia into the conflict. War between Austria and Serbia was one thing; it was the escalation that really mattered. One very de-escalatory thing the Germans did was to take no military action until very late in the crisis. On 5 July Wilhelm was asked whether he wanted the military to prepare for a European conflict, and he declined. Even after the Russians had mobilised, the Germans were still debating whether to declare a state of imminent danger of war, the last stage before mobilisation. Since mobilisation was such a big deal, the fact that Germany didn't mobilise until very late in the day was very de-escalatory. Perhaps if Russia had been as reluctant to mobilise, the whole war could have been avoided.

One thing, though, massively adds to German culpability: the invasion of France through Belgium. Countries can mobilise their militaries. They can even declare war. But what really matters is when soldiers actually start killing each other. Look at North and South Korea: technically at war for decades, but without actual military confrontation. The German invasion of France emphatically converted all the mobilisations and declarations of war into real bullets fired and real men killed. After that there was no backing down, and no way to put the conflict into a deep freeze.

The German invasion of France was the first major military offensive by a great power. Germany did it because it feared a two-front war against France and Russia at the same time, but in effect it meant attacking a country that wasn't really showing signs of attacking it. Would France have stayed neutral if Germany had focused only on Russia? There's no way of knowing, but I think it's a possibility. And by going through Belgium, Germany brought Britain into the war. Had Germany not invaded Belgium, there's a good chance Britain would have stayed out, which would likely have shortened the war and potentially reduced the death toll by millions.

### The Fischer debate

*[Watch from 29:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=37QFb-LRd9Q&t=1792s)*

It's impossible to talk about Germany's culpability for World War I without mentioning Fritz Fischer, the German historian who argued that Germany was primarily responsible. His work had a huge impact on the field and caused a great deal of controversy. In the words of another historian, Fischer "instantly rendered obsolete every book published previously on the subject of responsibility of the First World War and the German aims in that war".

Fischer argued that Germany had deliberately instigated the war in an attempt to become a world power. He pointed to what's known as the September Programme, a German plan to annex large parts of Europe and Africa if it won. His work was especially controversial in Germany, where the public believed Germany was not so culpable. Since then it has been criticised: historians point out that the September Programme wasn't produced until after the war began, and that the other powers also made plans for what they would do if they won, plans that also included some juicy spoils of war.

## France

*[Watch from 31:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=37QFb-LRd9Q&t=1865s)*

Overall, France played a relatively passive role throughout the July Crisis, assuming the assassination would at most lead to a local conflict. In fact, when it happened, it wasn't even on the front pages in France. The French were absorbed by a pretty bonkers scandal in which the wife of a former prime minister had killed a newspaper editor, because his paper had published love letters she had sent. So while all the countries to the east were melting down over the assassination, the French were busy just being French.

Because France played such a passive role, there aren't any real examples of it contributing to the escalation, which in my view decreases its culpability somewhat. If France can be criticised for anything, it's for not having done more to restrain Russia. France had been allied with Russia for decades and had the most influence over it. Even when Russia was coming out with the nonsense that Serbia wasn't involved in the assassination, or denying that Austria had any right to respond, France remained fully committed to the alliance.

France, like everyone else at the time, suspected that Serbia had some involvement. On 1 July Léon Descos, the French ambassador in Serbia, reported that the Serbian military was involved in the assassination and that Serbia was in the wrong. He was ignored. The French president, like the Russians, basically thought Austria had no right to do anything in response. That said, the French prime minister did tell the Russians not to do anything that would provoke Germany. Clearly that failed, as Russia was the first country to mobilise.

Germany demanded that France do everything it could to stop the Russian mobilisation, and France might have been able to if it had threatened to back out of the alliance. Essentially, Germany was demanding that France either declare neutrality in the coming war or face war with Germany. Given that the Russian mobilisation was such a huge escalation, perhaps that was a reasonable request. Either way, France didn't do it, and Germany felt it had no choice but to attack France.

Although France refused to declare neutrality, it might still have remained neutral if Germany hadn't attacked it. Would France have attacked Germany if Germany hadn't attacked first? We can never know for sure. Interestingly, French troops were actually pulled back from the German frontier as a demonstration of France's desire for peace.

## Britain

*[Watch from 33:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=37QFb-LRd9Q&t=2027s)*

Britain, like France, was barely involved for most of the July Crisis. Pretty much all the way through, it assumed it wouldn't fight in any coming war. Even at the beginning of August, just before the war started, most British politicians were acting on the assumption that Britain would stay out.

What eventually brought Britain into the war was the German invasion of Belgium, and even then it might have stayed out. Britain had signed a treaty in 1839 guaranteeing Belgian independence, but the cabinet took the view that it was a collective obligation of all the signatories. Even Churchill, such a hawk when it came to Hitler, was averse to defending Belgium: "I don't see why we should come in if they go only a little way into Belgium."

The British press was largely against war. The *Manchester Guardian* argued that Britain shouldn't get involved, famously stating that "Manchester cares for Belgrade as little as Belgrade cares for Manchester". The *Daily News* was disgusted at the idea of British lives being lost for, in its words, "Russian hegemony over the Slav world". Its editor, A. G. Gardiner, wrote an article titled "Why We Must Not Fight", arguing that there were no fundamental conflicts between Britain and Germany, and that crushing Germany would only establish a Russian dictatorship over Europe and Asia.

Given how determined Britain was to stay out of the conflict, there aren't really any examples of it contributing to the escalation. In fact, throughout the crisis Britain tried to get the situation put to a conference, in the hope that things could be talked out.

Britain can still be criticised for a couple of things. Like France, it could have done more to restrain Russia. The British knew Serbia was involved in the assassination and had some sympathy for Austria: the British foreign secretary acknowledged to the Austrian ambassador that Serbia was complicit, and that some of the demands in Austria's ultimatum were justified. Germany even tried to signal that if Britain remained neutral and kept France neutral, Germany wouldn't declare war and the fighting would stay in the east. Britain did nothing to act on that proposal.

Another potential criticism is that Britain's ambiguity may have made war more likely. Nobody knew whether Britain would get involved, which allowed France and Russia to talk themselves into thinking it would, and Germany to talk itself into thinking it wouldn't. If Britain had declared from the outset that it would get involved, that might have made the Germans more conciliatory. If it had said from the beginning that it wouldn't, that might have restrained France and Russia.

## So who was to blame?

*[Watch from 36:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=37QFb-LRd9Q&t=2188s)*

If those are the major actions of each nation, how can we compare them and judge each country's culpability? One thing that makes it so difficult is that almost every action was a response to another, so you're constantly faced with the question of whether a particular response was reasonable. How reasonable was it for Austria to declare war on Serbia after the assassination? How understandable, or forgivable? How reasonable was it for Russia to give Serbia its full backing? For France to keep backing Russia even as it mobilised? For Germany to give Austria its total backing?

These questions are impossible to answer in any objective way, and it seems to me that well-informed, dispassionate people can come to very different conclusions. That said, I'll give my view, though you should be advised it's of very little value. I would say that Serbia, Austria, Russia and Germany all deserve a degree more blame than Britain and France:

- **Serbia**, for the assassination of Franz Ferdinand and the provocations afterwards.
- **Austria**, for being so determined to go to war with Serbia.
- **Russia**, for mobilising so early.
- **Germany**, for emphatically kicking the war off by invading France.

I struggle to think of anything Britain or France did that was escalatory to the same degree.

Many historians prefer to reject the question of who was to blame for World War I altogether, and it's easy to see why. It really was a joint effort: a joint catastrophe.

## Be careful what you wish for

*[Watch from 37:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=37QFb-LRd9Q&t=2278s)*

It's difficult to know how to conclude something on such a sad but thought-provoking topic. While researching this, I came across many quotes that, in hindsight, sound extremely disturbing and distasteful: quotes from men who either wanted the war or had a cavalier attitude towards it. Austrians determined to humiliate Serbia and win back their pride. Serbians gleeful at the prospect of bringing down the Austrian Empire. Germans keen on the idea of fighting Russia because they thought it might be their last chance. Reading them with hindsight, I always had the same feeling towards these men: well, you got the war you wanted.

Perhaps, then, the message of World War I should be the old saying: be careful what you wish for, because you just might get it.
