*Adapted from the video [How did Japan end up so evil in World War Two?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Idai9lCQ5K8)*

Japan's behaviour in World War II can only be described as evil. The Japanese committed massacre after massacre, killing tens of millions of civilians. They forced Asian women into sexual slavery in military brothels. Perhaps most depraved of all were their human experimentation laboratories, where victims were injected with diseases or animal blood, and where some had limbs amputated so that doctors could see how fast they would die of blood loss.

All of that begs the question: how did Japan become so evil?

Germany's descent into madness is one of the most studied stories in history. So does Japan have a similar story? Was there a Japanese equivalent of Hitler who seemingly came out of nowhere and transformed the character of the nation? Or was Japan simply always like that: brutal and violent?

## The rise of the Japanese empire

*[Watch from 1:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Idai9lCQ5K8&t=68s)*

In the mid-1800s Japan was isolated from the rest of the world and governed by what was essentially feudalism. There was an emperor, but most political power was held by regional warlords who were constantly fighting each other. Japan was divided, obsessed with its own internal affairs, and had minimal contact with the outside world.

That changed in 1853, when an American naval expedition arrived at Tokyo and threatened war unless Japan opened up to trade. Recognising American military superiority, the Japanese agreed. For a proud people, being dictated to like that was intolerable, and the humiliation triggered a deep political crisis that transformed the country.

The constitutional revolution that followed became known as the Meiji Restoration. The regional warlords declined and Japan united around one central figure, the emperor. A constitution established a parliamentary system modelled on Britain's. Capitalism was adopted and industrialisation was rapid: within a few decades Japan went from a backward feudal state to an economic powerhouse with modern textile, shipbuilding and steel industries, and a military modernised along Western lines.

With that astonishing progress came confidence, even arrogance. Japan now believed it was superior to every other nation in Asia, and to prove it, it set out to humble the region's greatest power, China. China controlled Korea, a strategic bridge onto the Asian mainland that Japan could not tolerate in Chinese hands. War broke out in 1894 and, to everyone's surprise, Japan won in less than a year. Korea became independent of China, and Japan took control of Taiwan.

Expansion brought Japan into conflict with Russia, which was looking to take Korea and Manchuria. Japan actually wanted to avoid war, and proposed a deal in which Russia would take Manchuria and Japan would take Korea. In 1901 one of Japan's most important statesmen travelled to the Russian capital hoping to agree it. He was treated extremely badly, largely ignored, and left after months of humiliation. For years afterwards Japanese diplomats tried to get a meeting with the Tsar, and were treated with contempt. In February 1904 Japan gave up on diplomacy and launched a surprise attack on the Russian fleet at Port Arthur. Once again everyone expected Japan to lose, and once again it outclassed its enemy on land and sea. In less than two years Russia was forced to sign a humiliating peace. Japan was now the dominant power in East Asia, and in 1910 it formally annexed Korea.

## From British ally to rogue empire

*[Watch from 5:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Idai9lCQ5K8&t=344s)*

Japan's transformation drew the attention of the other world powers, and Britain made an alliance with it. That mattered in World War I, when Japan played an important, often forgotten role seizing German colonies in Asia. But the territory it was awarded afterwards was small, and that bred resentment of the West.

In 1921 a pro-Western prime minister was assassinated, beginning two decades of political instability in which 20 governments came and went, most lasting less than a year. Politics was increasingly dominated by extremists and militarists who wanted more expansion in Asia, and in 1931 they got their wish. Japan had had troops in Manchuria, a resource-rich territory in the north-east of China, since 1905. By now a group of generals had taken full control of that army and were acting independently, and without any authorisation from Tokyo they annexed the entire region. When the government tried to stop them, another prime minister was assassinated in 1932. From then on it was clear that the military, not the politicians, held the real power in Japan.

Japan grew more and more extreme. Faced with international condemnation, it left the League of Nations. In 1936 it allied with Nazi Germany, and the following year it launched a full-blown invasion of China, quickly taking much of the coast and the major cities. The invasion of China was the point of no return, the moment that cemented Japan's descent into evil.

In 1941 Japan attempted to dominate all of Asia and the Pacific. It attacked the American navy at Pearl Harbor, making an enemy of the United States, then invaded much of the rest of Asia, including Malaysia, the Philippines, Thailand and Indonesia. Very quickly it ruled an empire of about 400 million people. Over the next few years America's island-hopping campaign pushed Japan back, capturing one strategic island after another. Faced with the prospect of a costly invasion of the Japanese mainland, in August 1945 the United States dropped nuclear bombs on two Japanese cities, forcing Japan to surrender and give up its empire. In total, Japan's excursion into Asia cost about 30 million lives.

## What Japan actually did

*[Watch from 9:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Idai9lCQ5K8&t=578s)*

The cruelty was vast, and much of it is not well known.

- **Human experimentation.** The most notorious laboratory was Unit 731, where roughly 300,000 people died. Victims were injected with diseases or animal blood, deliberately dehydrated, or operated on without anaesthetic. Limbs were amputated to see how fast people would bleed to death. Some prisoners had their stomachs surgically removed and their oesophagus reattached to their intestines. Flamethrowers were tested on people tied to stakes, and victims were placed in low-pressure chambers until their eyes burst from their sockets. The victims included children and even babies.
- **The Three Alls policy.** "Kill all, burn all, loot all." Finding it impossible to distinguish civilians from soldiers in China, the Japanese army's solution was to kill every Chinese person it found, even those who were clearly civilians.
- **Massacres.** In the Nanking Massacre, one of the worst war crimes of the century, roughly 200,000 civilians were murdered over six weeks, often in the most sadistic ways, alongside the mass rape of Chinese women. Babies were thrown into the air and caught on bayonets. In one infamous story, two Japanese soldiers competed to see who could be the first to kill 100 people. The atrocities weren't confined to China: in Manila, the capital of the Philippines, up to half a million people were massacred, with Filipino women and children used as human shields and then murdered.

Between 1942 and 1945 about 400 million people lived under this regime, and the number of deaths was truly staggering. It is impossible to know exactly how many people the Japanese killed, but it might have been as many as 30 million.

So why was Japan so astonishingly evil?

## The long-term causes: a warrior culture and a divine emperor

*[Watch from 11:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Idai9lCQ5K8&t=716s)*

Was Japan's brutality rooted far back in its history, or was it a short-term phenomenon? To put it another way: if World War II had happened two or three decades earlier, would Japan have behaved so horrifically? As is so often the case, I think it was a mix of both.

Japan's actions were partly due to beliefs and values that had been part of its culture for centuries. One was the glorification of warriors, above all the samurai, who were both an elite warrior class and the ruling nobility. At the heart of the samurai tradition was Bushido, a strict moral code that prized loyalty, courage, honour and a willingness to die for one's cause. Those values can be noble, but in Japan they fostered a culture in which violence was glorified.

By the 1900s the samurai no longer existed, but the remnants of their warrior value system lingered, and they clashed with the liberal ideas Japan was adopting from the West. The Bushido ideal of self-sacrifice for the emperor sat uneasily beside the Western ideal of a free citizen in a democratic society. Liberal ideas were welcome while Japan was prospering, but in the hard interwar years Japanese society returned to its old traditions, loaded with false notions of greatness and the glorification of violence.

Another traditional idea was that the emperor was more than a man. In Shinto, Japan's traditional religion, he was a direct descendant of the sun goddess Amaterasu, and that divine lineage gave him enormous symbolic power. His divine status became the cornerstone of national ideology, and in the early 1900s Japan's leaders used it to justify military aggression. The annexation of Korea and Manchuria and the invasion of China were all framed as fulfilling a sacred mission. In a sense they were holy wars, almost crusades, and crucially, all means were allowed.

In some ways, Japan's attitudes towards war and brutality were simply typical of Asia at the time. In China, the Mandate of Heaven held that any man could claim to be the legitimate ruler if he could defeat the current one. Asia as a whole had a value system similar to the one Europe had held thousands of years earlier, where might is right and strength is valorised. Europe, influenced by Christianity, had since moved away from those attitudes, towards notions such as the duty of the powerful to the weak and the need for rulers to have the consent of the governed. In Asia there was far less of that sentiment.

## Why that can't be the whole story

*[Watch from 15:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Idai9lCQ5K8&t=925s)*

We can be sure those long-term causes weren't the whole story, because Japan hadn't always behaved so badly in war. Only three decades earlier, during the Russo-Japanese War, it behaved with nothing like the same brutality. One Western journalist wrote:

> The chivalry displayed by the Japanese towards a fallen foe has been demonstrated on innumerable occasions since the momentous crossing of the Yalu. I have witnessed the considerate treatment of the Russian prisoners, treatment which could not be excelled in point of consideration by any army in the world.

In World War I, too, German prisoners of war were treated very well. They apparently played football matches not only among themselves but against local club teams, went on weekly excursions and put on theatrical productions.

The contrast between that civilised Japanese army and the one of World War II is astonishing. So what changed? In short: repeated humiliation created a huge amount of anti-Western resentment, that resentment destabilised Japanese politics, and the instability allowed the military to take far too much power.

## Humiliation and resentment

*[Watch from 16:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Idai9lCQ5K8&t=1019s)*

The first humiliation was being forced at gunpoint to open up to trade in 1853. It was followed by a series of exploitative treaties that gave Western powers trade privileges, control over ports and even the ability to ignore Japanese laws. The Japanese saw them as a symbol of their subjugation, and the anger made Japan determined to industrialise and modernise as fast as possible, to show the world it wasn't inferior. That worked fairly well: by 1900 Japan had a more equal relationship with the other powers and had even renegotiated some of the humiliating treaties.

But the resentment didn't go away. In fact it got worse after World War I. At Versailles, where the great powers discussed the League of Nations and the post-war order, Japan made a very reasonable request: a racial equality clause, which would ensure that the West treated all nations equally regardless of race. The European powers rejected it. Not only was it a diplomatic defeat, it reinforced the Japanese perception that they were viewed as racially inferior.

More humiliations followed. The 1922 Washington Naval Treaty, an agreement between the five major naval powers on the size of their navies, restricted Japan to a navy only 60% the size of Britain's or America's. Two years later the United States passed an Immigration Act that prevented immigration from Japan and the rest of Asia, which the Japanese took as a message that they weren't welcome in the West.

That resentment had a profound effect on Japanese politics. The most obvious example came in 1921, when Prime Minister Hara Takashi was assassinated. Hara was relatively liberal, Japan's first prime minister not to come from the aristocracy, and known to be pro-Western and in favour of international cooperation. He was killed just as he was preparing to agree the Washington Naval Treaty. Nationalists and elements of the military were angered by his willingness to compromise and saw it as a betrayal of Japan's interests. His assassination showed that those who favoured aggressive, isolationist policies were willing to use violence to get what they wanted.

## A democracy that tilted to the right

*[Watch from 20:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Idai9lCQ5K8&t=1205s)*

Throughout the 1920s Japan was in an unstable transition from the old political order to a more modern democratic system. It had had some of the structures of democracy for a while: the Meiji Constitution of 1889 created an elected House of Representatives, but only the richest 2% of men could vote. Suffrage gradually expanded, and in 1925 all men were given the vote.

That might sound like Japan was liberalising, but it happened almost too fast and provoked a backlash. As political power was devolved to the people, the conservative establishment grew nervous about new left-wing groups, and passed the Peace Preservation Law: an explicitly anti-left law that criminalised any movement trying to alter Japan's established political order. Socialist, communist and left-wing groups, which had begun to gain traction in the 1920s, were now excluded from political life, and anyone deemed a threat to the existing system could be arrested. The effects were particularly stark in Korea, where colonial authorities used the law to suppress any resistance or nationalist sentiment. Korean independence activists were arrested, imprisoned or even executed.

Together, universal male suffrage and the Peace Preservation Law skewed politics further and further to the right. More people could vote, but the range of options available to them had been severely restricted. Nationalist and conservative parties that favoured a strong, authoritarian state came to dominate, and liberal movements advocating social reform were marginalised.

Economic problems made politics more extreme still. In 1927 a series of bank failures led to bank runs, and the government's strict response sent unemployment and poverty soaring. Then came the global crash of 1929 and the Great Depression. Japan was hit particularly hard: in only two years economic output fell by a whopping 18%, worse than in Britain, the United States, France or even Germany. The effects on the population were catastrophic, and just as in Germany, the mainstream parties were discredited and politics became more extreme.

## The biggest reason: a slow military takeover

*[Watch from 23:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Idai9lCQ5K8&t=1394s)*

The resentment, the political instability, the marginalisation of the left: all of these help explain why Japan went so crazy in World War II. But in some ways their role was to set the stage for the biggest reason of all. Japan was taken over by its military. By the mid-1930s it was essentially a military dictatorship, and the generals had stopped paying any attention to the civilian government in Tokyo. With that power, the military went rogue. It became uncontrollable and did whatever it wanted, and the culmination was the atrocities of World War II.

It started in Manchuria. Ever since the war with Russia, Japan had had influence there, including a small military presence, and had even helped the Manchurian ruler, Zhang Zuolin, come to power. Through the 1920s they had a mutually beneficial relationship: Zhang let Japan operate significant infrastructure such as railways and coal mines. But in the late 1920s he grew closer to the Chinese nationalists and tried to reduce Japanese influence, and the Japanese forces in Manchuria came to see him as a threat. What they wanted was for Japan to occupy the region outright. The government in Tokyo disagreed, worried that deeper involvement would be expensive and chaotic. That disagreement began a years-long power struggle between the government and the military.

Frustrated with both Tokyo and their Manchurian partner, the Japanese forces devised a ruthless and cynical plot: assassinate Zhang, create chaos in Manchuria, and force Tokyo to accept that Japan had to get more involved. They acted entirely on their own initiative. In June 1928 a Japanese soldier planted a bomb on a bridge, which exploded as Zhang's train passed. Zhang died a few days later.

The assassination didn't get Tokyo to do what the military wanted. Instead it caused a political crisis. The prime minister admitted that Japanese troops were responsible and promised to prosecute those involved, which provoked a backlash from the conservative establishment and the military: an investigation, they argued, would make Japan look terrible and might even force it to withdraw from Manchuria. In the end the prime minister backed down, and a few months later he was forced to resign. The military was emboldened, and over the next few years it took more steps to increase its power over the civilian government:

- **November 1930.** A prime minister who had just negotiated a new naval treaty with America and Britain, seen by the military as a betrayal, was shot and later died of his wounds.
- **March 1931.** A group of young army officers from a secret society attempted a coup to install a military dictatorship, believing it was the only way to deal with the Depression and restore Japan's international prestige. The coup failed, but the plotters received only very mild punishments.
- **September 1931.** In its most daring plot of the whole period, the army in Manchuria staged an explosion near a Japanese outpost, blamed nearby Chinese troops, and used it as an excuse for a full-scale invasion. It was a classic false-flag operation, and the military had started a war with China entirely without authorisation from Tokyo. Within days Japanese forces had killed hundreds of Chinese soldiers and taken a lot of territory. The government hesitated and failed to condemn it; when it did call for a halt, the army simply refused.

What the Manchurian army did next was even more shocking. It demanded that the government support its occupation, and threatened that if it didn't, the army would declare independence from Japan. It actually had a declaration ready, which said:

> It may be necessary for members of the Army in Manchuria committed to our cause to temporarily renounce their Japanese nationality in order to achieve the rapid advancement of our aims.

That was probably a bluff, but it threw the government into complete chaos. All the while the Manchurian army kept advancing, defeated the local Chinese warlord and moved on the Manchurian capital. Tokyo ordered it to halt and withdraw. With relations now extremely hostile, the stage was set for another coup attempt. Again it failed, and again the punishments were extremely mild, even milder than before. The government's inability to show any authority led to its fall in December, as the army in Manchuria captured the capital.

1932 was the civilian government's last chance to claw back some power. A new, experienced prime minister was appointed with the aim of regaining control of the military and ending the war with China. No prizes for guessing what happened to him. He was assassinated, and with him civilian governance in Japan effectively ended. There had been a slow military coup over the course of years. The power struggle was over, and the military had won.

## The men who ran the war

*[Watch from 30:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Idai9lCQ5K8&t=1818s)*

The atrocities Japan committed in World War II were a direct result of that military takeover. The Manchurian army in particular developed an especially brutal and evil culture. Its success raised its prestige, and its men became some of the top commanders when Japan invaded the rest of China and Asia. They oversaw atrocities like the Nanking Massacre. They also played a key role in Japan's non-military atrocities: the human experimentation at Unit 731 was carried out by a clandestine division of the Manchurian army. And they infiltrated key positions in what remained of the civilian government. One of them became Prime Minister of Japan from 1941 to 1944, and another served as Minister of War.

## So why was Japan so evil?

*[Watch from 31:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Idai9lCQ5K8&t=1888s)*

Many different things contributed, but for me the most important was the takeover by the military. Japan came to be controlled by the most brutal, psychopathic subsection of its society. The men who came to run the show weren't ordinary members of the military, but the Manchurian army, a particularly ruthless group that, through numerous assassinations, violence and intimidation, accrued total power over Japan. They then turned Japan into something totally alien to what it had been just a few decades earlier.

What might have happened to Asia if the Japanese empire had continued to dominate it for decades doesn't bear thinking about. We must all be grateful to those who fought to bring about its end.
